What a million tokens costs on your own GPU

There are three ways to get a million tokens out of an open model: buy them from an aggregator, rent a card and serve them, or serve them on a card you already own. They are not close. Here is the arithmetic, with every input printed beside the answer.

Quick answer

A million output tokens from a 27B-class open model costs you about $0.09 in electricity on an RTX 4090 you already own, $0.20 on a rented spot A5000, and $0.28 to $0.45 to buy from an aggregator. Own the card and you are three to five times under the market.

§1 · THE FORMULA

One line, and three numbers you control.

Serving is a rate business. A card costs money by the hour whether or not a request is in flight, and it produces tokens only while one is. So the price a station has to charge to cover itself is the hour’s cost divided by the tokens that hour actually produced - adjusted for the platform fee, because an operator keeps 70% of what a request is charged and the remaining 30% pays for routing, metering, settlement and the payout rails.

The third input is the one people skip, and it dominates everything. Duty is the share of the hour actually spent producing tokens - a card that is busy half the time serves half the tokens for the same rent, so its break-even price doubles. Every table here gives full duty and half duty side by side for exactly that reason.

The number that moves the answer most is throughput. The figures below assume a dedicated card batching concurrent requests: 120 tok/s aggregate for an 8B, 200 for a 20B mixture-of-experts, 150 for a 27B dense model at 4-bit. Those are achievable and they are assumptions, not measurements of your machine. Halve them and every price here doubles, which is why the last section is about nothing else.

§2 · THE CARD YOU ALREADY BOUGHT

The purchase price is gone. Only the power bill counts.

A card you own has no hourly rental. What it has is a marginal draw: the extra watts it pulls while generating, over what it was already pulling sitting idle. At the US average of about $0.15/kWh, that is a remarkably small number, and it is the whole reason a home station can undercut a data centre.

§3 · THE CARD YOU RENT

Spot pricing changes the answer completely.

You do not have to own a card to run a station. The GPU rental market prices interruptible capacity at roughly half its on-demand rate, and interruption is not the problem here that it is for training: a station that vanishes is a station the router stops sending work to, and the next request goes elsewhere. §5 · RENTING CAN PAY

There is real margin in spot capacity, if you make the card faster.

Owning is cheapest, but renting is not automatically a loss. Take the smaller cards, price under the aggregator, and the arithmetic works: an 8B vision model on an interruptible 3060 breaks even at $0.10 per million at half duty, while the market charges $0.455 for the same class. Charge $0.35 - comfortably under - and you keep $0.245 against a $0.10 cost.

§6 · THE LEVER

Optimisation is not a hobby here. It is the margin.

Every number on this page divides by tokens per second, which means anything that makes the card generate faster cuts your break-even by the same proportion. A card that goes from 150 to 300 tok/s does not get 5% better; its cost per token halves.

  • Batch, do not queue. Serving one request at a time leaves most of a modern GPU idle. A server that batches concurrent requests can multiply aggregate throughput several times over with no change to the weights, and it is the single largest lever on this list.
  • Quantise deliberately. Four-bit weights move a 27B model into 24GB and cut the memory traffic that dominates generation. The question is never “is it lossless” but “is the loss visible on the work you actually serve”.
  • Speculative decoding. A small draft model proposing tokens for a large one to check can pay for itself handsomely on predictable text, and not at all on hard text. It is worth measuring rather than assuming.
  • Mind the KV cache. Long contexts spend memory bandwidth on attention rather than on generating, so a station that advertises a huge window and serves long prompts is quietly slower than its own benchmark.
  • Pick the right model for the card. A model that spills out of VRAM is not a slower station, it is a different order of magnitude.

We wrote a book about exactly this - Inference on the Edge, quantization, speculation and the physics of local models, free to read. The concurrency numbers behind the batching point are in How many people can one GPU serve?

§7 · THE ASYMMETRY

Dense models come home. Sparse ones stay in the data centre.

One row in the market table does not fit the pattern: gpt-oss-20b at $0.13, cheaper than models a third of its size. It is a mixture-of-experts with roughly 3.6B parameters active per token, so a data centre can serve it at enormous batch sizes for very little memory traffic. That is a scale advantage, and scale is the one thing a home station does not have.

The mirror image is the dense models. A 27B dense model reads all 27B parameters for every token, which is memory bandwidth, which is exactly what a single big card in somebody’s house is good at and what a shared data-centre GPU is expensive at. That is why an owned 4090 undercuts gemma-3-27b by two and a half times and cannot touch gpt-oss-20b.

So choose your band deliberately. If you are putting a card on the air to earn, dense models and vision models are where a home station wins. Leave the sparse mixtures to the people with a thousand of them.

§8 · CHECK IT

Run the numbers against your own card.

None of this is worth taking on faith, and none of it is meant to be. The formula in §1 needs three inputs you can measure in an afternoon: what your card draws at the wall while generating, what your utility charges, and what your server actually produces under load rather than in a single-stream benchmark.

The calculator on the pricing page does the arithmetic for you from your own numbers, on both sides: what a card would earn, and what you are paying today re-priced against the live band. Every rule it uses is printed on that page.

§9 · QUESTIONS

The ones people ask first.

Does this count the price of the card?
No, and deliberately. These are marginal costs: what one more million tokens costs on hardware whose purchase is already a sunk decision. If you are choosing whether to buy a GPU in order to serve, the rental columns are the honest comparison, because that is the business you would be entering.
Why is the platform's 30% in the formula?
Because an operator keeps 70% of what a request is charged, so covering a $0.07 hour needs $0.10 of charges, not $0.07. Leaving the fee out would understate every break-even price on this page by about 43%.
Is 150 tokens a second realistic for a 27B model?
As an aggregate across concurrent requests on a dedicated 24GB card at 4-bit, yes. As a single-stream number, no - single-stream on that hardware is more like 35 to 45. The distinction matters enormously, because a station serves several people at once and it is the aggregate that pays the rent.
What happens to my numbers if my electricity is expensive?
They scale linearly. At $0.30/kWh rather than $0.15, every owned-hardware figure here doubles: a 4090 goes from $0.09 to $0.18 at full duty. Even doubled, it still sits under most of the market table.
Does an interrupted spot instance break anything?
Not for the network. A station that disappears stops being routed to, and requests go to another station serving the same model. Interruptible capacity is a reasonable way to run a station precisely because the system already assumes machines come and go.
Where do the comparison prices come from?
OpenRouter's public model API, read on 31 August 2026, output-token list prices, with the full model ids printed so they can be checked. Rental prices are Vast.ai's live board the same day. Both move; the arithmetic does not.
