What a million tokens costs,
and what you can sell it for

You can rent a GPU for a cent and a half an hour. You can sell what it produces on an open market. Those two prices are nowhere near each other, and the gap is the whole business.

The trade

Rent an interruptible RTX 3060 for $0.015/hr. Serve an 8B model on it. Busy half the time, a million output tokens costs you $0.10 to cover, and the going rate for that model is $0.455. Charge $0.35, stay well under the market, and you are taking 3.5× what the hour cost you.

§1 · THE SPREAD

Rent by the hour. Sell by the token.

A rented card costs the same whether or not a request is in flight, and it produces tokens only while one is. So the price you must charge to cover yourself is the hour's rent divided by the tokens that hour actually produced, grossed up for the platform fee: you keep 70% of what a request is charged, so covering a $0.07 hour needs $0.10 of charges.

Duty is the share of the hour actually spent generating. It is the input people skip and it moves everything: a card busy half the time serves half the tokens for the same rent, so its break-even doubles. Rental rates below are Vast.ai's interruptible board on 31 August 2026.

§2 · THE LEVER

Throughput is the margin. There is no second lever.

Every number here divides by tokens per second, so anything that makes the card generate faster cuts your break-even by the same proportion. A card that goes from 150 to 300 tok/s does not get 5% better: its cost per token halves, and the margin is the part that grows. That is the entire distance between the row that loses money above and the row that clears 1.75×.

  • Batch, do not queue. Serving one request at a time leaves most of a modern GPU idle. A server that batches concurrent requests multiplies aggregate throughput several times over with no change to the weights. Largest lever by a wide margin, and the cheapest.
  • Quantise deliberately. Four-bit weights move a 27B model into 24GB and cut the memory traffic that dominates generation. The question is never "is it lossless" but "is the loss visible on the work you actually serve".
  • Keep the context honest. Long prompts spend bandwidth on attention rather than on generating, so a station advertising a huge window and serving long inputs is quietly slower than its own benchmark, and quietly less profitable.

The figures above assume a dedicated card batching concurrent requests: 120 tok/s aggregate for an 8B, 200 for a 20B mixture, 150 for a 27B dense model at 4-bit. Those are achievable and they are assumptions, not measurements of your box. Measure yours under load rather than single-stream, and put your own number in.

§3 · WHICH BANDS PAY

Dense and vision models pay. Sparse mixtures do not.

You are pricing against what the aggregators charge for the same open weights. From OpenRouter's public model API, output tokens, 31 August 2026:

gpt-oss-20b at $0.13 is cheaper than models a third its size because it is a mixture-of-experts with roughly 3.6B parameters active per token, so a data centre serves it at enormous batch for very little memory traffic. That is a scale advantage, and scale is the one thing you do not have. A 27B dense model reads all 27B parameters per token, which is memory bandwidth, and one card does that as well as anyone's. Take the dense and vision bands. Leave the sparse mixtures to the people with a thousand GPUs.

§4 · OR SKIP THE RENT

If the card is already yours, the rent is zero.

Everything above pays a landlord. If you own the card, the purchase is a sunk decision and the only cost is the extra watts it pulls while generating. At $0.15/kWh that is not a small edge, it is a different order of magnitude:

§5 · RUNNING ONE

Four steps, whoever owns the card.

  1. Get a card. An interruptible instance on Vast.ai, or the one under your desk. Pick it to fit the band you chose in §3, not the other way round: a model that spills out of VRAM is not a slower station, it is a different order of magnitude.
  2. Serve it. Any OpenAI-compatible server - vLLM, llama.cpp, Ollama, LM Studio, whatever you already run. Turn continuous batching on.
  3. Put it on the air. roger share finds the server you are already running. The connection is outbound only, so there are no ports to open on a rented box.
  4. Price it under the market. roger share --price-in 0.18 --price-out 0.30. You keep 70% of every request; the router does the rest.

On a rented box those four steps are one paste into the instance’s ONSTART box - curl -fsSL https://rogerai.fm/vast-onstart.sh | bash - which installs the client, brings the model up, waits for the weights to load and goes on air. It is free by default, so no secret of yours sits on a machine you do not own, and it refuses to price a share on a box you have not signed into rather than quietly serving for nothing. The knobs are in the manual.

An interrupted spot instance breaks nothing: a station that disappears stops being routed to, and the request goes to another one serving the same model. The network already assumes machines come and go, which is exactly why interruptible capacity is a reasonable thing to build on.

§6 · QUESTIONS

The ones people ask first.

How much can you make renting a GPU and serving models?
An interruptible RTX 3060 rents for about $0.015 an hour. Serving an 8B model at 120 aggregate tokens a second, busy half the time, a million output tokens breaks even at $0.10 including the 30% platform fee. The market rate for that class is $0.455, so charging $0.35 leaves 3.5 times cover. Double the throughput and the break-even halves.
Is renting a GPU to serve on RogerAI actually allowed?
Yes. A station is a machine running a model; nothing cares whose data centre it sits in or whether the hour was rented. Price it, serve it, keep the same 70%.
Why is the platform's 30% inside the break-even?
Because you keep 70% of what a request is charged, so covering a $0.07 hour needs $0.10 of charges, not $0.07. Leaving the fee out puts every break-even here 30% too low.
Is 150 tokens a second realistic for a 27B model?
As an aggregate across concurrent requests on a dedicated 24GB card at 4-bit, yes. Single-stream on that hardware is more like 35 to 45. The distinction is the whole article: a station serves several people at once, and it is the aggregate that pays the rent.
Does an interrupted spot instance break anything?
Not for the network. A station that disappears stops being routed to, and the request goes to another station serving the same model. Interruptible rental works here precisely because the system already assumes machines come and go.
What if nobody sends me work?
Then duty falls and the trade falls with it, which is why duty is a column here rather than a footnote. On rented hardware the rent runs whether or not requests do, so start on an hourly instance you can drop, measure your real duty, and size the commitment to it.
