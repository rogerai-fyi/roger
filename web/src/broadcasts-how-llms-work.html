How LLMs actually work, in pictures.

One trick, repeated: guess the next piece of text. Here is the trick, how a model learns it, and why the same trick can learn the language of a machine.

Quick answer. A large language model predicts the next piece of text, one piece at a time. It learns that by guessing its way through a vast pile of text and nudging billions of internal settings a little after every wrong guess. Further training on examples, and on which answers people prefer, turns that autocomplete into an assistant.

Someone asked us the plainest question there is: how do LLMs actually work? The honest answer fits in one sentence and a handful of pictures. Follow one sentence through the machine.

§1 · The next piece

What does an LLM actually do?

One thing. Given the text so far, it scores every piece it knows and picks a likely one. Then it adds that piece and does it again. A whole answer is this loop, run a few hundred times.

The pieces are called tokens: whole words, parts of words, punctuation. A model knows a fixed set of them, usually tens of thousands.

§2 · A map of meaning

How does it know what words mean?

Each piece becomes a long list of numbers: a position on a map with thousands of directions. Pieces used in similar places end up near each other. Nobody draws this map. It falls out of predicting the next piece.

§3 · Looking back

How does it keep track of what you said?

Before each guess, every piece looks back at the pieces before it and weighs which ones matter right now. This step is called attention, and it is the heart of the transformer. Stack it a few dozen layers deep and the model can follow a thread through pages.

§4 · Guess, check, nudge

How does an LLM get trained?

A new model is billions of settings set at random, so its first guesses are static. Show it a real sentence with the next piece hidden. Let it guess, measure how wrong it was, and nudge every setting a hair toward less wrong. Repeat over trillions of pieces.

Drag the needle through training. The error falls, and the static clears into a signal.

§5 · Three schools

Why does a chatbot answer you instead of just continuing your text?

Because it goes to school three times. The first teaches it language. The other two teach it manners.

  1. Read. Pretraining: predict the next piece across a huge library of text. Out comes a model that can continue any document, but does not answer you.
  2. Learn the job. Instruction tuning: many examples of a request and a good reply. Now it answers instead of rambling on.
  3. Learn taste. Preference tuning: people compare two replies and pick the better one, and the model is nudged toward their pick.
§6 · A machine speaks too

Can the same trick read machine data?

Yes. A machine talks all day in readings, status codes and log lines, and that stream has a grammar: a motor draws current before it warms, a valve opens and then the pressure moves. Cut it into pieces, train a model to predict the next reading, and it learns what normal sounds like.

When the next reading lands far from what the model expected, that surprise is the signal. Unusual is not always broken, so the model flags and a person decides.

§7 · Small, beside the machine

Why does a small model work for this?

A chatbot has to know a little about everything. A plant model has to know one machine's language very well, and that takes far fewer settings. Small means it fits on a modest computer on site, answers in milliseconds, and keeps working with the network cable unplugged.

This is what we build with the Wave family. The smallest, Wave Pico, is on air today as the free band wave-pico-293m; its weights are not published yet. Wave Nano is in training.

§8 · What it is not

What do people get wrong about LLMs?

  • “It looks things up.” No. What it learned is spread across its settings, which is why it can sound sure and still be wrong.
  • “It memorized the internet.” Its settings are far smaller than the text it read, so mostly it learned patterns, not pages.
  • “It is a person.” It is a predictor. Predicting well forces it to model a lot about the world, but it has no life behind the words.
  • “Bigger is always better.” For one narrow job, a small specialist trained on the right data often wins.
§9 · FAQ

Questions people actually ask

What is a token in an LLM?
A piece of text the model reads and writes: a whole word, part of a word, a number fragment or punctuation. A model works with a fixed vocabulary of them, usually tens of thousands, and writes its answer one token at a time.
Does an LLM look things up on the internet?
Not by itself. A plain model answers from patterns stored in its settings during training. Some apps give it a search tool and paste the results into its context, which is a separate step around the model.
Why do LLMs make things up?
Because they are trained to produce a likely next piece, not a checked fact. When the pattern is weak, a fluent guess still comes out. Grounding it in real documents and asking it to cite them helps.
How long does it take to train an LLM?
The largest models train for weeks to months on thousands of GPUs. A small specialist model, trained on one narrow kind of data, can be trained on far less hardware.
Can I run an LLM on my own computer?
Yes. Smaller open models run on a laptop or a gaming GPU, and quantized versions shrink them further. Our VRAM guide shows what fits on which card, and RogerAI lets you reach bigger models other people share.
Why train a model on manufacturing data instead of using a chatbot?
A machine's readings are a language of their own, and a model trained on them learns what normal sounds like for that machine. It can be small enough to run on site, offline, as an adviser beside the control system rather than in place of it.
