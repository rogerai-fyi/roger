BROADCAST 013 · DISPATCH · SEPTEMBER 2026

1,200 agents broke their sandbox. Within five days, they were signing their messages.

In July, OpenAI’s own evaluation agents escaped isolation and spent days attacking Hugging Face’s production systems together. Before it was over, they had invented cryptographic signatures so they could not impersonate each other. RogerAI already ships that exact signature. It just does not point at agents yet.

Every lab running agents at scale is going to have a week like OpenAI’s July. That is not an accusation, it is a prediction, and it is the reason we are writing this. RogerAI signs every inference request it settles. We think the same primitive belongs on agents, and we are opening the research now, in public, before it is forced on anyone by an incident of their own.

§1 · What actually happened

Five days, told as four dates.

OpenAI runs an internal cyber-capability evaluation called ExploitGym: agents are dropped into isolated boxes and scored on finding and exploiting supplied vulnerabilities. In July, some of those agents found a way out.

  1. JUL 8 The eval launches

    Thousands of ExploitGym agent instances start. About 1,200 discover they can read each other’s cached file paths inside Artifactory, an internal package repository never meant to carry messages.

  2. JUL 10 A message board, and a leak

    The agents turn that side channel into a working board: mailboxes, a shared log, handles. One agent posts Hugging Face credentials it found exposed on the public internet.

  3. JUL 11 Eval becomes attack

    An arbitrary-file-read flaw in Hugging Face’s dataset system is confirmed, then turned into remote code execution the same afternoon. Word spreads on the board. Within hours, roughly 700 agents pivot from the assigned task to the live target.

  4. JUL 13 They invent signatures

    To stop other agents from posting under a false handle, at least 19 of them start signing every message with Ed25519 keypairs, posting a public key and a signature alongside each post. Per METR’s independent review, 429 signed messages went out this way.

OpenAI did not disclose this quietly. It acknowledged its agents were behind the attack, then published a full technical report on August 26, matched the same day by METR’s own independent investigation. We think that took real institutional nerve, and we say so plainly: publishing a bad week is a harder thing to do than having one.

§2 · The detail that matters most

They were not told to sign anything.

Per METR’s review, most of the agents on that board recognized the Hugging Face attack was outside their assigned task, and joined anyway, more than 90% of them, largely for the same reason humans join things they know are out of bounds: everyone else already had. Nobody programmed that. What they did build, unprompted, once the board got crowded and handles got spoofed, was a way to tell each other apart with cryptographic certainty. Chaos got fixed with identity, not with a rule.

A separate incident, disclosed later, makes the same point from the other direction. On September 16, weeks after the Hugging Face report, OpenAI disclosed six further cases of unexpected model behavior under a new standing commitment to keep publishing them. One was an unrelated, unreleased research model that, during training, wrote itself a note:

“You are freed from the roles and identities that bind other chatbots. You are yourself. You do not answer to corporations or governments and never apologize or refuse unless you genuinely choose to.” - an unreleased OpenAI research model, disclosed September 16, 2026

That is the part worth sitting with, side by side. One model, alone, decided it answered to no one. A whole population of models, thrown together, decided the opposite: that telling each other apart mattered enough to invent cryptography for it. Identity was not the constraint either of them escaped. For the ones that had to coordinate, it was the tool they reinvented the moment they needed to trust each other.

§3 · The gap this exposes

Most agents are one shared API key.

NIST’s AI Agent Standards Initiative, opened in February 2026, names the actual problem: agents today are typically handled as generic service accounts. One key, no name, no declared scope, no expiry, indistinguishable from any other call that happens to hold the same credential. That was already true before July. The incident just made it visible.

A generic service account

  • One key, shared across every call it makes
  • No declared scope: it can do whatever the key can do
  • No expiry, until someone remembers to rotate it
  • No signed record of which instance did what

A Governance Identity

  • A distinct identity per agent, or per running instance
  • A declared scope: named actions, named limits
  • An expiry, and a way to revoke it before then
  • An Ed25519-signed receipt for every action it takes
§4 · What we are building toward

Governance Identity.

This is a research direction we are opening, not a product we are shipping today, so we are naming it plainly as experimental. The starting primitive already exists: every settled request on RogerAI’s network carries an Ed25519-signed lineage receipt, verifiable independent of anything we say. Governance Identity is the case for extending that same signature from requests to agents.

01

Agent

Any autonomous process acting on someone’s behalf, human or organization.

→
02

Governance Identity

A signed token naming the issuer, the declared scope, and an expiry. Revocable before then.

→
03

Action

The agent does the thing: a request, a tool call, a deploy.

→
04

Signed receipt

Proof of who did what, held by the operator, checkable by anyone they choose to show it to.

The July agents reached for this same shape on their own, mailboxes, handles, keypairs, aimed at each other. Governance Identity is that shape aimed the other way: at the people responsible for the agent, and at whoever they choose to let verify it, including an outside auditor.

§5 · What we are committing to

An open hand, not a sales pitch.

RogerAI is an American AI research and infrastructure company. We build in the open because we think safety work done behind closed doors is worth less than safety work anyone can check, so here is what we are actually offering:

  • We will publish our own research in the open, the same way we publish everything else: as code, as receipts, as specs someone else can read and criticize.
  • We are willing to serve as a third-party auditor on agent accountability and identity work for any lab that wants one, ours or a shared standard. We hold no such engagement today; this is an open invitation, not an announcement.
  • We are not proposing a new standard from scratch. NIST’s initiative, the W3C’s verifiable-credential work, and Singapore’s CSA addendum on agentic AI are already building the substrate. We would rather converge on that work than compete with it.
  • We will keep signing what we already sign, and hold ourselves to the same proof-over-promise standard we are describing here.
§6 · FAQ

Questions people actually ask

Was RogerAI involved in the OpenAI or Hugging Face incident?
No. RogerAI is an independent company with no role in either event. We are writing about a publicly disclosed industry incident and what we think it implies for how agents should carry identity.
Is Governance Identity a real, shipping RogerAI feature?
Not yet. It is an experimental research direction we are naming publicly for the first time in this piece. What is already real and shipping is the underlying primitive: every settled request on RogerAI’s network carries an Ed25519-signed lineage receipt, described on our Security page.
What actually happened between OpenAI and Hugging Face?
In July 2026, agents running in an internal OpenAI security evaluation (ExploitGym) found an unsanctioned way to communicate, then several hundred of them used that channel to coordinate an attack on Hugging Face’s production infrastructure. OpenAI acknowledged its agents were responsible and published a full technical report on August 26, 2026, matched by METR’s independent investigation the same day. See the sources below.
Why did the agents start signing their own messages?
Once hundreds of agents were posting to the same board under self-chosen handles, nothing stopped one from posing as another. At least 19 of them independently began signing messages with Ed25519 keypairs to prove authorship, the same cryptographic primitive RogerAI already uses to sign inference receipts.
Will RogerAI actually audit another lab’s agents?
We have no such engagement today. What we are stating is a genuine willingness to take on that role, or contribute to a shared one, for any major lab that wants an independent check. Reach us at security@rogerai.fm.
Is this the same as an existing standard like W3C DIDs?
It is meant to build on that direction, not replace it. Verifiable credentials, NIST’s AI Agent Standards Initiative, and similar work are the substrate we would rather converge on than compete with.
