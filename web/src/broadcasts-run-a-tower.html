Run a Tower - and get paid for the carrying

Until now there were two jobs on this network: ask, or answer. There is a third one now. A Tower is the mast in the middle - it runs no model, is never handed a key to the traffic, and earns a cut of everything that crosses it. Or point the same binary inward and run a private relay network for your own local models, with nothing leaving the building.

Quick answer. A Tower is a relay you run on your own Linux box. Consumers submit sealed work to its hub; providers' nodes poll that hub, serve the work, and seal the answer back. The Tower is on that path twice and is handed a key to it neither time. Every settled request splits 90% to the serving node, 5% to you, 5% to the platform - your 5% lands on the same Stripe payout rail as any other earning. Or initialize the same binary standalone and it becomes a private relay network for your own local models - its own trust root, loopback by default, no RogerAI lookup, no telemetry, and no earning either. Install it with curl -fsSL https://rogerai.fm/install.sh | ROGERAI_COMPONENT=tower sh.

Updated September 2026: the split above improved to 90/5/5 when the platform fee dropped to 10% across the network (it was 70/10/20 when this broadcast first aired). Stations and Towers both earn from the same requests as before; the platform simply takes less.

A decentralized network has an awkward middle. Someone has to stand between the person asking and the machine answering: hold the endpoint open, hand work to whoever has capacity, and carry the answer back. On most networks that middle is one company's server rack, and calling the result decentralized is a stretch. The Tower is our attempt to make that middle something other people can own - and to pay them for it, because a job nobody is paid to do is a job that quietly becomes ours again.

§1 · The third job

What is a Tower, exactly?

A Tower extends the broker's data plane. It hosts the hub: the place consumers submit sealed work to, and the place providers' nodes poll to pick that work up. A provider does not have to do anything special to reach you, and increasingly will not get to choose: a node registers with the broker the ordinary way, is probed and scored the ordinary way, and the relay it serves through is Core's decision. Attaching carries the node's keys, its model, and its own per-token price.

What a Tower is not is as important. It runs no model - there is no GPU requirement, and a Tower never answers a prompt. It is not a proxy you point at your own hardware; the nodes that attach are other people's. And it is not a validator, a stake, or a token - there is nothing to buy and nothing to lock up. It is a relay that carries sealed parcels and gets paid per parcel.

  • Station - someone's GPU, running a model, answering. Earns 90%.
  • Tower - the relay carrying sealed work between them. Earns 5%.
  • Core - authorizes the spend up front and settles the receipt afterwards. Keeps 5%.
§2 · What it can see

What does a Tower see?

No, and that is a property of the design rather than a promise about our conduct. The consumer authorizes the spend at Core, then seals the request to the node's session key and hands the sealed bytes to your hub. Your Tower queues ciphertext. The node polls, opens it, serves it, signs a receipt for the token counts, and seals the answer straight back to the consumer. Your Tower carries that back too - still ciphertext - and forwards the signed receipt to Core for settlement.

So a Tower operator sees traffic shape: which station, how many bytes, when. Not the prompt, not the answer. It is never handed a key to the content. That distinction is the whole guarantee: this is not a rule an operator is asked to keep, it is a key they were never given, and only the second kind survives contact with somebody who would rather not keep it. It is also why running a Tower carries less exposure than running a station - you are not choosing to keep someone's words off your disk, because they were never on it in a form you could keep.

§3 · The money

What does a Tower earn?

Ten percent of every request your Tower carries, settled. The number it is ten percent of is the thing worth being precise about: it is the serving node's own listed price for the tokens it actually served, as recounted and resolved by Core - not a rate we set, and not a rate the node can inflate at settlement. The same request pays the node 90% and leaves the platform 5%.

It pays out like everything else. Relay earnings ride the same rail a serving node's do - the same 30-day hold with its 90-day reserve tail, the same $25 minimum, the same Stripe Connect onboarding, the same cash-out button on your payouts page. They are listed there separately as relaying rather than serving, which is provenance only; the money is identical and comes out of the same balance.

be realistic

The mechanism is live end to end, but traffic through any one Tower is early, and it is worth being exact about why rather than leaving you to guess. Two things have to happen before a single request crosses your relay, and neither is something you control:

  1. Providers have to be on the relay fabric. They no longer opt in - an ordinary roger share offers a signed-in node to the fabric on its own, and there is no flag to type. But it is best effort: a node with no signed-in account, or one that cannot reach a relay, simply keeps serving the ordinary way and never reaches you. So your traffic still depends on how much of the fleet is both signed in and reachable, not on consumer demand alone.
  2. Core has to send them to you. A node does not choose its Tower - Core assigns one, and today that assignment is first-fit among live, admitted Towers advertising a data-plane endpoint, with no weighting by load, health or distance. In practice an established Tower can absorb attachments while a newer one waits. Locality- and load-aware placement is designed and being built; until it lands, which Tower gets a given node is close to arbitrary.

So: expect your relay figure to start at zero and to move with those two things rather than with the day you installed. Run a Tower because you want this middle to belong to more than one party; treat the 10% as upside on that, not as a projection.

§4 · The other reason

Can I run one privately, for my own models?

Yes, and it is the mode that shipped first. A Tower is initialized as one of two kinds, chosen once and never changed in place:

  • joined - a child relay of the public network. Core stays the admission, routing, settlement and revocation authority, consumers are routed to you, and this is the mode that earns the 10%.
  • standalone - your own local network with its own trust root. It performs no RogerAI discovery, settlement or advertisement, and that is structural rather than a setting you could flip: a standalone Tower's config will not even accept a public authority URL, an enrollment token, or a payout setting.

A standalone Tower is the answer to "I have four machines with models on them and I want one endpoint in front of all of them, without any of this leaving the building." It binds to loopback by default, performs no RogerAI DNS lookup, sends no telemetry and runs no update check. roger-tower login refuses outright in this mode, and says why: this Tower is standalone and needs no account - nothing it does leaves this machine. Stations on a private network get reachability they do not have on their own, and the whole thing is governed by your trust root rather than ours. 

$ roger-tower init --dir /var/lib/roger-tower --mode standalone
the trade Standalone earns nothing. Not "not yet" - a private network has no settlement path to RogerAI by construction, and its configuration rejects payout settings outright. Pick standalone because you want a private relay for your own hardware; pick joined because you want to carry public traffic and be paid for it. One data directory is one mode for life: switching means initializing a new one, and nothing is copied across.
§5 · Bringing one up

What do I need, and what are the commands?

A Linux box with a stable address and a port you can expose - amd64 or arm64. No GPU. A Tower is a long-running server process, which is exactly why it ships for Linux only: macOS and Windows machines run the Station or the client, not a Tower. Install it with the same one-liner as the client, with the Tower component selected: 

$ curl -fsSL https://rogerai.fm/install.sh | ROGERAI_COMPONENT=tower sh

The installer verifies the release checksum before it installs anything, and refuses outright on macOS or Windows rather than leaving you a binary that cannot serve. Then five commands take you from nothing to on air: 

$ roger-tower login                     # same account as the website - GitHub, Apple, or email
$ roger-tower init --dir /var/lib/roger-tower --mode joined   # keys + local state (see §4)
$ roger-tower doctor                    # pre-flight: config, reachability, clock
$ roger-tower register                  # enroll this Tower against the network
$ roger-tower serve --hub :8444 --relay-public YOUR.HOST:8444

Enrolling is a signed act tied to your account, because putting a relay on the network under your name is an account decision - that is what the login step is for, and it is also what makes the 10% yours. You attach nobody by hand - and cannot: Core decides which Tower each node lands on (see the note in §3 for what that means for a new Tower). From there, roger-tower status shows admission state and attached nodes, roger-tower earnings shows what you have carried, roger-tower probe --model NAME drives the whole sealed loop as a consumer to prove your Tower actually carries work, and roger-tower station revoke is your kill switch for a node serving under you. A hardened systemd unit ships in the repo under packaging/tower/ - dynamic user, ProtectSystem=strict, a dedicated state directory - so the relay comes back on boot.

§6 · Why bother

Why does the network need other people's Towers?

Because the alternative is that we are the only road. Every local model somebody puts on this network - the 3090 in a spare room, the Mac mini under a desk, the rack in a university lab - is only reachable if something in the middle is holding the door open. If that something is exclusively ours, then the interesting part of the network is decentralized and the boring, load-bearing part is not, and the boring load-bearing part is the one that fails politically rather than technically.

Towers spread that. More of them means more paths between the person asking and the machine answering, in more places, under more names, on more jurisdictions' terms. It also means the people keeping local models reachable are the people being paid to - which is the only version of this that survives the day we stop subsidizing it.

The concept page is The Tower, and the exact operator runbook is §T of the manual.

§7 · FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a GPU to run a Tower?
No. A Tower runs no model and answers no prompt - it carries sealed work between consumers and the nodes that do answer. A modest Linux box with a stable address and an exposed port is the whole requirement.
What can a Tower operator see?
Traffic shape, and nothing more: which station, how many bytes, when. The consumer seals the request to the serving node's session key, so the Tower queues ciphertext and the answer comes back sealed the same way. The key to the content is never handed to the Tower in either direction, which is why the guarantee does not rest on the operator's restraint.
How much does a Tower earn?
5% of every settled request it carries, measured against the serving node's own listed price on Core's recount. The same request pays the node 90% and the platform 5%. Traffic through any one Tower is still early, so treat the figure as demand-driven rather than as a projection.
How and when do relay earnings pay out?
On the same rail as serving earnings: a 30-day hold (10% reserved to day 90), a $25 minimum, Stripe Connect onboarding, and the same cash-out button on the payouts page. Relay earnings are listed separately as relaying rather than serving, but the money is identical.
Which platforms can run a Tower?
Linux on amd64 or arm64, by design. A Tower is a long-running server process; macOS and Windows machines run the Station (serve a model) or the client instead.
Do I have to recruit nodes to attach to my Tower?
You cannot, and that cuts both ways. Core, not the provider and not you, decides which Tower a node attaches to. Assignment is currently first-fit among live admitted Towers advertising a data-plane endpoint, with no weighting by load, health or distance, so a new Tower may sit idle while an established one takes attachments; locality-aware placement is designed and being built. roger-tower station revoke removes a node serving under you if you need it gone.
Can I run a Tower privately, just for my own machines?
Yes - initialize it with --mode standalone and it is a private relay network with its own trust root. It binds to loopback by default, performs no RogerAI discovery, advertisement or settlement, sends no telemetry and runs no update check, and its configuration refuses public-network and payout settings outright. The trade is that a standalone Tower earns nothing, by construction rather than by policy. A data directory is one mode for life; switching means initializing a new one.
Is there a stake, a token, or hardware to buy?
No. There is nothing to buy and nothing to lock up. You install a binary, sign in with the account you already have, and enroll.
How do I know my Tower is actually carrying work?
Run roger-tower probe --model NAME. It drives the entire sealed loop as a real consumer would, end to end through your own hub, so a green probe means the path works rather than that the process is up. roger-tower status shows admission state and attached nodes.
